## The thesis

An outcome costs what it takes of its owner's attention, and no more outcomes can be accepted than the owner can stand behind. Two things the owner holds keep AI useful, cost-effective and proper: the definition of done and the power to stop.

The author has said it more plainly, in his LinkedIn posts of November 2026:

> "That is Lean AI: the power to intervene, improved by AI and not overwhelmed by it. AI should take busywork away from the Human, not manufacture more of it to check."

> "Headcount is what a good design leaves, not what it targets. **Lean AI is not about headcount. It is about a better process.**"

The thesis sentence puts those lines in terms that can be counted. What AI manufactures for its owner to check is paid for in the owner's attention, and that is the cost. The power to intervene is overwhelmed when the checking a workflow demands is more than the hours its owner can give, and that is the ceiling. It is improved when the definition of done and the checks built into each step leave the owner only the cases that need a person.

The title gives the order of the work. **Slow AI**, as the doctrine canon states it, is a precondition and not a speed limit. Lean AI is the practice that follows it. The paper puts the order in one line: the power to stop comes first, and then only the outcomes that someone will accept.

Lean here is lean in the sense of the Toyota Production System, as the paper's Section 9 reads it. It is not a headcount target, the cheapest model or a promise of lower total cost.

## The arithmetic

The module counts what one accepted outcome costs: model and tool charges, the effort of producing the work and the effort of overseeing it, for every case and every attempt, divided by the outcomes accepted under a definition of done fixed in advance. The paper counts the oversight that a named owner's workflows demand and sets it beside the qualified hours that owner can give. The oversight effort in the first count is the demand in the second, priced at the owner's rate. The same minutes sit in both.

Two consequences follow, and the book is an attempt to work them out.

1. **The cost of an accepted outcome is mostly attention.** In the module's illustration the reviewer's minutes carry the cost and the model does not. A definition of done that turns acceptance into tests which pass or fail, and a check that runs before a person reads, lower that cost without lowering the diligence.
2. **The owner's hours are a ceiling.** Past them, work can still be produced, and it can no longer be accepted by someone who has read it. A workflow that stops itself when it leaves its boundary is what lets the owner's hours go to the cases that need a person.

Both are hypotheses. The figures behind them so far are the invented ones in the module's illustration and in the paper's worked example. The Lean Tiny Servants are where they are tested: each reports what share of its cost per accepted outcome was oversight, and each test record is kept, failures included.

## Who it is for

The named owner of a workflow, first. Then the architect who builds the workflow and the finance reviewer who is asked what it costs. The author's first book, [*Final Liability Rests with the Human*](https://github.com/rolldabones/final-liability-rests-with-the-human-book-wip), argues that a named human answers for what AI does. This one is about keeping the capacity to answer when the work is delegated.

## Contents

The module defines its subject in one sentence: "Lean AI is the disciplined removal of avoidable work, inference and dependency from AI-enabled workflows, while preserving defined outcomes, required controls and human accountability." The book unfolds that sentence. Chapters 2 to 4 take what is preserved and chapters 5 to 7 what is removed. The titles are working titles.
